Photoelectric effect

Einstein equation, threshold and stopping potential

1. The puzzle for classical waves

Monochromatic light ejects electrons at once if the frequency is high enough. A classical wave predicts “brighter light → larger KE” and “dim low-frequency light will emit if you wait long enough”. Neither is true.

2. Einstein’s photoelectric equation

Light arrives as photons. One photon gives energy hf to one electron; after paying the work function Φ, the leftover is the maximum kinetic energy.

\( hf = \Phi + K_{\max} = \Phi + eV_s \)
Photon energy
\( E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} \)
Stopping potential
\( eV_s = K_{\max} \)

3. Threshold frequency and the K–f graph

Emission occurs only for f > f₀. Kmax versus f is a straight line whose slope is Planck’s constant h, x-intercept f₀ and y-intercept −Φ.

\( f_0 = \dfrac{\Phi}{h},\quad K_{\max} = h(f - f_0) \)

Intensity changes current only

Photon rate ∝ intensity, so the saturation current Is rises with brightness. Each photon’s energy is unchanged, so Kmax and Vs stay fixed.

Everyday examples

Phototubes, automatic-door sensors, old exposure meters. A solar cell uses a semiconductor PN junction — related, but not the metal work-function model.

Observation steps

Threshold, intensity, then Vs

  1. 1Pick “below threshold”: red light on sodium. In the energy well, hf is shorter than Φ — no electrons, I = 0.
  2. 2Switch to “blue light” or slide f past f₀. Check Kmax = hf − Φ. Zinc needs UV; visible light is not enough.
  3. 3Increase intensity only: I rises in proportion, but Kmax and Vs stay the same. That rules out “brighter wave → faster electrons”.
  4. 4Apply reverse voltage up to Vs so I falls to 0. On the Kmax–f graph, log several points; the slope should be close to h.
